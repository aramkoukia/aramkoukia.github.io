If I had to pick the most important thing I do as a manager, I don't think it would be planning, or hiring, or design reviews. It would probably be 1:1s. It's the one meeting on my calendar that is entirely about the other person, and it's also very easy to waste.

I've run a lot of these over the years and changed my approach a few times. The most recent change came partly from AI tools and partly from just noticing, after enough years, which conversations actually mattered and which ones I could have skipped.

## No status updates

This is the rule I hold most firmly. If a 1:1 turns into "here's what I worked on this week," we've both lost half an hour. Status is available in the team's tools, in standup, in written updates, and these days I can ask an assistant to summarize what someone shipped, reviewed, and got blocked on in a few seconds. It's cheap information.

The 1:1 is for the expensive stuff: what someone is worried about, where they want to grow, what's frustrating them, and the things they'd never say with the rest of the team in the room. When someone opens with status, I'll usually say something like "I saw the update, looks like it's moving. How are you doing with it?" and see where that goes.

## Their agenda goes first

We keep a shared doc and they add topics before I do. Mine go at the bottom, and some weeks we never get to them, which is fine.

Some people come with a full list every week. Others never write anything down. For the quiet ones I keep a few questions in my back pocket:

- "What's the most annoying part of your week right now?"
- "If you could change one thing about how the team works, what would it be?"
- "Is there anything I'm doing that makes your job harder?"

The last one is awkward to ask, and it took me a while to get comfortable with it. It also gets the most useful answers, though usually not the first few times. People need to see that you actually want to hear it before they'll say anything real.

## What we end up talking about

Not every week looks the same, but over a month or so I try to touch on a few kinds of conversations.

The first is just how someone is doing. I mean them, not the project: energy, workload, stress, whatever is going on outside work. Most weeks this takes two minutes. Some weeks it takes the whole meeting, and I've learned not to rush it. Nobody has an AI model for "my kid hasn't slept in a week and I can't think straight," and nobody should have to explain that in standup.

Then there's growth. Where do you want to be in a year, what are you trying to get better at, what would make the work more interesting? This has come up a lot more lately, because plenty of people are quietly wondering how their job is going to change. I'd much rather have that conversation out loud. I wrote about [why star players quit](/2017/12/why-your-teams-star-players-quit/) years ago, and not growing was near the top of that list. A 1:1 is usually where you notice it first.

Feedback goes both ways. I try to give at least one specific piece every couple of weeks, and specific means something like "the way you asked about the failure mode in Tuesday's design review changed the outcome," rather than "nice work lately." I also ask for feedback on myself, regularly, and try hard not to get defensive when I get it.

And then context. Why a decision got made, what's coming next quarter, how their project fits into the bigger picture. A surprising amount of frustration on teams comes down to people missing a piece of context that their manager had and assumed everyone else did too.

## Talking about the fear

This one is newer, so I want to call it out separately.

A lot of engineers are anxious about what AI means for their careers, and most of them won't bring it up, least of all with their manager. It feels risky to admit. So every so often I bring it up myself: how are you feeling about the AI tooling, is it making your work better or worse, is there anything you're worried about?

I try to be straight about what I don't know, which is quite a lot. I can still share what I'm seeing, which skills I think are becoming more valuable, and how their role is changing on our team specifically. In my experience people cope with uncertainty fine. Silence from their manager is much harder on them.

## Smaller habits

I try very hard not to cancel. Rescheduling is fine, but canceling sends a pretty clear message about where someone ranks. I take notes in the shared doc so we can both see them, and I follow up on things from previous weeks, because remembering what someone told you three weeks ago does more for trust than almost anything else I know of. For harder topics I sometimes suggest a walk, since some conversations are easier side by side. Most people get weekly; a few senior folks prefer every other week with a bit more depth, and it's worth just asking. And if we've covered what matters in fifteen minutes, I give them the time back.

The tools now handle most of the information-passing that used to fill up these meetings, and I'm grateful for that. It leaves the half hour for the parts of the job that were always the reason to have the meeting in the first place.
